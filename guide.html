HIPA

How it works · a plain-language guide

← Back to the tool GitHub
The one-line idea: HIPA is a witness protection program for medical records. The patient gets a new name, new address, and new ID numbers, but their story stays the same: the diagnosis, labs, medications, and timeline. A student learns exactly the same medicine, but nobody can trace it back to the real person.

1. Why the text never leaves your device

If a cleaning tool sent your text to a server to be cleaned, you would be sending raw patient data across the internet in order to protect it. That's like mailing your house key to a locksmith so they can tell you whether your door is secure.

HIPA runs entirely inside your web browser. The page includes a security rule (Content-Security-Policy: connect-src 'none') that forbids the browser from sending anything anywhere, even if someone changed the code. You can test it: load the page, turn off Wi-Fi, and it keeps working. Patient text is never saved. Only your settings (like "tags vs. fakes") are remembered.

2. What HIPAA counts as an identifier

HIPAA's Safe Harbor method lists 18 kinds of identifiers. Remove all of them (and have no reason to think the rest could still point to someone), and the text counts as de-identified. Think of it as a checklist at airport security: every item on the list must be taken out of the bag.

#IdentifierWhat HIPA does
1Names (patient, relatives, employers, household members)Realistic fake name, same sex, same everywhere
2Geography smaller than a state: street, city, county, ZIPFake street and city, state kept, ZIP cut to first 3 digits (or 000 for small areas)
3All date parts except the year, and ages over 89Dates shifted; ages 90+ become 90+
4–5Phone and fax numbersFake numbers in the fictional 555-01xx range
6Email addressesFake @example.com address
7Social Security numbersFake number starting with 9 (never issued as a real SSN)
8–11Medical record, health plan, account, certificate and license numbersRandom number in the same format
12–13Vehicle IDs (VIN, plates) and device serial numbersRandom number in the same format
14–15Web URLs and IP addressesReplaced with reserved example addresses
16–17Biometrics and full-face photosNot applicable to text (labeled biometric IDs are replaced)
18Any other unique number, code or characteristicUnlabeled long numbers replaced; unusual details flagged for you

Kept exactly as written: sex, diagnoses, symptoms, labs, vitals, medications and doses, procedures, clinical reasoning, the state, the year, and ages 0–89.

3. The two tricky rules

Age: kept, except for the oldest patients

Very few people are 97 years old, so in a small town "a 97-year-old woman" can point to one person. HIPAA therefore requires ages 90 and above to be grouped together as 90+. Because a date of birth would reveal the same thing, HIPA also hides the DOB when the patient is 90 or older.

94-year-old female. DOB 05/22/1930. → 90+ year-old female. DOB [DOB withheld: age 90+].

Dates: slide the whole calendar, don't delete it

If every date were deleted, the medicine would stop making sense ("troponin rose on ___ and fell on ___"). Instead, HIPA picks one random amount of time per document and moves every date by that same amount, like sliding a transparent calendar across the page. A 4-day stay is still 4 days. The amount is always whole weeks, so "Monday" stays a Monday.

Admitted 03/01/2024, discharged 03/05/2024 → Admitted 11/03/2023, discharged 11/07/2023 (still 4 days)

4. Consistency: one person, one fake name

A novel whose main character changes name every chapter would be impossible to follow. HIPA keeps a private lookup table for each document so that John Smith, Mr. Smith, J. Smith and SMITH, JOHN all become the same fake person. Family members keep a shared surname, and fake first names keep the original sex, so "he/she" still matches.

5. How identifiers are found: three layers of security

Layer 1: the metal detector (patterns). Anything with a fixed shape: phone numbers, SSNs, emails, dates in a dozen formats, ZIP codes, record numbers. Fast and very reliable.

Layer 2: the trained officer (context). The words around something give it away. The word after Dr., Patient:, his wife, lives in or MRN: is almost certainly an identifier.

Layer 3: the sniffer dog (dictionaries). Lists of thousands of first names, surnames and cities catch names that appear with no clue nearby. Once a name is found anywhere, every other mention of it is caught too.

There's also a medical allow-list, so that names that are part of medicine stay put: Parkinson's disease, Bell's palsy, Foley catheter, Babinski sign, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, Wilson disease. HIPA checks the word that follows, so "Dr. Wilson" is replaced but "Wilson disease" is not.

6. The colors: like spell-check for privacy

Spell-check underlines problems, but you make the final decision. HIPA does the same:

  • Green: replaced, with strong evidence.
  • Yellow: replaced, but found without strong context. Glance at it.
  • Orange: not replaced, but could identify someone in context (a famous job, "the only case in the state", a holiday date, a newsworthy accident). You decide.

Click any highlight to see the original or restore it. Select any plain text in the result to redact it. Use Always replace for things like a pet's name or a small business, and Never replace for false alarms.

7. Hidden identifiers to watch for

Some details aren't on the list of 18 but can still give a person away when combined. These are called quasi-identifiers, like a jigsaw puzzle where no single piece shows the picture but a few together do:

  • Rare disease + small town ("the only FOP patient in Wyoming")
  • Distinctive jobs ("the town's mayor", "NFL quarterback")
  • Newsworthy events ("injured in the Main St bridge collapse")
  • Unusual details: tattoos with names, a pet's name, a family business

8. Limitations (please read)

  • No automated tool can guarantee 100% de-identification. HIPA is a strong first pass. A human still needs to read the result before sharing it.
  • It's designed for English, US-style clinical text. Names written all in lowercase, very unusual names with no context, and non-US address formats are harder to catch.
  • It covers the Safe Harbor method. The other HIPAA method, Expert Determination, requires a qualified statistician.
  • It handles text only. Images, scanned PDFs and photos must be handled separately.
  • Before putting patient-derived text into any cloud or AI service, follow your organization's rules. Many institutions require approval from a privacy office or IRB even for de-identified data.
  • The Replacement key table links fakes back to real people. It is PHI. Never share or save it.

9. Tips for best results

  • Keep labels like Patient:, DOB:, MRN:. They help detection.
  • Read the yellow and orange items. They're where mistakes are most likely.
  • Use Tags mode when you want it obvious that data was removed, and Realistic mode when you want natural-reading teaching cases.
  • Click Generate new fakes to get a different set of names and a different date shift.
